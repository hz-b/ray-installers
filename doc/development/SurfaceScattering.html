    Surface errors and aperture diffraction of mirrors¶

    A ray tracer sees a mirror's height errors only through the tilt they give the local normal, and nothing diffracts at a mirror's edges. For X-ray mirrors of good quality both simplifications can be wrong by large factors, in opposite directions and at opposite ends of the photon-energy range. This note describes two per-mirror settings that replace them with a wave-optically correct treatment: the physics, the implementation, how to use them, and what they do not cover.

    setting (RML id) values what it does
    Profile Treatment (surfaceErrorTreatment) geometric (local slope) (0, default) · wave-optical (PSD scattering) (1) how a surface profile file acts on the rays
    Edge Diffraction (Fraunhofer) (edgeDiffraction) off (0, default) · on (1) Fraunhofer diffraction at the mirror aperture

    Both settings exist for the plane mirror, cylinder, cone, sphere, toroid, ellipsoid, hyperboloid, paraboloid, elliptical toroid and the experts optics, and for the plane, spherical and toroidal gratings (section 2.4). Crystals, capillaries and the reflection zone plate keep the geometric treatment. A file without the settings traces exactly as before.

    1. Where the geometric model goes wrong¶

    The QMAP beamline at BESSY III was simulated in RAY-UI and, independently, with the wave-optics code SRW, with ideal mirrors up to the exit slit and the same measured-like surface profiles on the two focusing mirrors M5 and M6 (horizontal and vertical FWHM, 0.07 % photon band in both codes):

    400 eV RAY-UI 400 eV SRW 1000 eV RAY-UI 1000 eV SRW
    exit slit, vertical 15.00 µm 15.10 µm 15.00 µm 15.09 µm
    focus, horizontal 142.3 nm 59.7 nm 161.8 nm 42.0 nm
    focus, vertical 94.3 nm 86.7 nm 104.9 nm 94.8 nm

    Up to the exit slit, where no mirror carries a profile, the codes agree. The horizontal focus differs by a factor of 2.4 to 3.9, and the difference is generated at M5 and M6. The geometric width hardly depends on the photon energy; the wave-optical one falls with the wavelength.

    The reason is the size of the phase error. A height error h imprints on a beam reflected at grazing angle theta the phase

    phi = 2 k h sin(theta) = 4 pi h sin(theta) / lambda
sigma_phi = 4 pi sigma_h sin(theta) / lambda          (rms)

    The M6 profile has sigma_h = 0.25 nm at theta = 4°, which gives sigma_phi = 0.07 rad at 400 eV and 0.18 rad at 1000 eV. A wavefront with 0.1 rad of phase error is almost perfect: its Strehl ratio exp(-sigma_phi^2) is 0.99 to 0.97, the focus keeps its diffraction-limited core, and a few percent of the power go into a faint, wide halo. Geometric optics instead deflects every ray by twice the local slope, however small the height behind that slope, and turns the same surface into a blur of the full slope width. That is the limit sigma_phi >> 1, and it does not apply here.

    At low energy the opposite happens. M6 is overfilled up to about 200 eV, its 200 mm length limits the numerical aperture, and the focus is the Fraunhofer pattern of the mirror aperture. A ray tracer has no diffraction at a mirror edge and returns a focus below the diffraction limit (at 50 eV RAY-UI gives 160 nm where the diffraction limit of M6 alone is about 270 nm).

    2. Scattering from a surface with height errors¶

    2.1 The surface as a phase screen¶

    In the Kirchhoff (tangent-plane) approximation the reflected field is the incident one times exp(i phi(x, z)). For a surface whose heights are a stationary Gaussian random process with autocovariance C(tau) = <h(r) h(r + tau)> and variance sigma_h^2 = C(0), the angular distribution of the reflected intensity is the Fourier transform, over the separation tau, of the surface transfer function

    H(tau) = < exp(i [phi(r + tau) - phi(r)]) > = exp( -sigma_phi^2 [1 - C(tau) / sigma_h^2] )

    This is the Beckmann–Kirchhoff result [1], in the form Harvey and co-workers use for optical surfaces [2, 3] and Sinha et al. derive for X-rays at grazing incidence [4]. Spatial frequency f on the surface corresponds to a change of direction by the grating equation (section 2.3).

    2.2 The expansion that becomes a Monte Carlo¶

    Expanding the exponential,

    H(tau) = exp(-sigma_phi^2) * sum_{n >= 0} (sigma_phi^2)^n / n! * [C(tau) / sigma_h^2]^n

    The Fourier transform of C(tau) / sigma_h^2 is the power spectral density normalised to unit integral, p(f) = PSD(f) / sigma_h^2, which is a probability density; the transform of its n-th power is the n-fold convolution of p with itself. The reflected intensity is therefore a Poisson mixture: with probability

    P(n) = exp(-sigma_phi^2) (sigma_phi^2)^n / n!

    the ray carries the sum of n spatial frequencies drawn independently from p(f). This is the algorithm RAY-UI uses, ray by ray. It contains the familiar limits:

    • n = 0, weight S = exp(-sigma_phi^2): the specular core, reduced by the Debye–Waller or Strehl factor. 1 - S is the total integrated scatter of Bennett and Porteus [5].
    • sigma_phi << 1: after the core, n = 1 dominates, and the halo has the shape of the PSD. This is first-order (Rayleigh–Rice) scattering [6, 7].
    • sigma_phi >> 1: the sum of many frequencies is Gaussian, with covariance sigma_phi^2 <f f>. Section 2.3 shows that this is exactly the geometric deflection by twice the slope.

    The variance of the direction change is sigma_phi^2 <f^2> lambda^2 for every sigma_phi: the rms deflection is always twice the rms slope. What changes with sigma_phi is the shape. A small phase error keeps most rays undeflected and sends a few far out; a large one deflects all rays a little. The rms width of a focus therefore hardly changes between the two treatments, while its FWHM and its encircled energy can change by a large factor.

    2.3 Direction change at grazing incidence¶

    A spatial frequency (f_x, f_z) of the surface, z along the mirror and x across it, adds lambda (f_x, f_z) to the tangential components of the ray's unit direction vector. That is the grating equation of that Fourier component:

    cos(beta') = cos(beta) + lambda f_z      -> delta_beta = -lambda f_z / sin(theta)   (in the plane of incidence)
sin(alpha') = lambda f_x                 -> delta_alpha = lambda f_x                (across it)

    In the plane of incidence the change is amplified by 1 / sin(theta); across it, it is not. In the limit sigma_phi >> 1 the rms tangential change is

    lambda sigma_phi sqrt(<f_z^2>) = 4 pi sigma_h sin(theta) sqrt(<f_z^2>) = 2 sin(theta) m_z

    with m_z = 2 pi sigma_h sqrt(<f_z^2>) the rms meridional slope. In the plane of incidence that is a deflection of 2 m_z, and across it 2 sin(theta) m_x: exactly the geometric rules, including the well-known suppression of sagittal slope errors at grazing incidence.

    2.4 Gratings¶

    A height error h of a grating moves its grooves along the normal. The incoming wave and the diffracted order then travel h sin(alpha) and h sin(beta) further, with alpha and beta their grazing angles, so

    phi = k h (sin(alpha) + sin(beta)),   sigma_phi = 2 pi sigma_h (sin(alpha) + sin(beta)) / lambda

    For a mirror, alpha = beta, and this is the formula of section 1. The rest is unchanged. A spatial frequency f of the surface adds lambda f to the tangential direction of the diffracted ray: the grating equation of that Fourier component, on top of that of the grooves. The table, the Poisson expansion and its limits are those of sections 2.1 to 2.3; only mu takes both angles. A ray sent into the zeroth order has alpha = beta and gets the mirror formula.

    Edge diffraction on a grating is diffraction at the aperture the diffracted order sees, L sin(beta) in the dispersion plane. Through the dispersion it is the resolving power of the grating. At fixed incidence the grating equation cos(alpha) - cos(beta) = m N lambda gives sin(beta) d(beta) = m N d(lambda), with m the order and N the line density. The first zero of the Fraunhofer pattern, d(beta) = lambda / (L sin(beta)), is therefore d(lambda) = lambda / (m N L). That is Rayleigh's lambda / d(lambda) = m N L, the order times the number of illuminated grooves [12]. A ray tracer without it resolves as finely as the source size and the slope errors allow. As on a mirror, the setting belongs on a grating that the beam overfills; an underfilled grating's resolution is already in the divergence of the rays.

    3. Implementation¶

    The code is src/raycore/surface_scattering.f90. RAY.FOR calls it at four places.

    3.1 The table of a mirror¶

    When SURF has read a profile file for a mirror whose slope errors are on and whose Profile Treatment is wave-optical, SURFSCAT_SETUP builds a table:

    1. It removes piston and the best-fit plane; they belong to the alignment, not to the surface error.
    2. sigma_h is the rms of the residual.
    3. It applies a Welch window along both axes and zero-pads to the next power of two at least twice the profile size. Without the window, the edge discontinuity of a non-periodic measured map would leak into the high frequencies and appear as a spurious halo.
    4. The periodogram |FFT|^2 without its DC term, normalised to one, becomes a cumulative table over the frequency grid, together with the second moments <f_z^2> and <f_x^2>.

    3.2 Each reflected ray¶

    After REFLEX, SURFSCAT_APPLY computes, from the photon energy and the local grazing angle, mu = sigma_phi^2.

    • For mu <= 50 it draws n from a Poisson distribution with mean mu. It then adds n frequencies from the table, each placed uniformly within its frequency bin.
    • For mu > 50 it draws the sum directly from the Gaussian with variance mu <f^2> per axis.

    It adds lambda f to the tangential part of the direction and restores the unit length along the normal. A frequency that would make the ray evanescent leaves it specular; at grazing incidence that needs spatial periods shorter than the wavelength and does not occur for measured maps.

    PLANES does not tilt the normal for such a profile, so the slope of the profile is not counted twice. The rms slope error (slopeErrorSag, slopeErrorMer) keeps the geometric treatment: an rms slope does not fix the heights, and the heights are what sets sigma_phi.

    The grating routines PLGRAT, SPGRAT and TOGRAT call SURFSCAT_APPLY_GRATING after REFRAC, with the sine of the incoming grazing angle taken before the diffraction. It uses the mean of the two sines in mu. SURFSCAT_APPLY is its mirror form, both angles equal.

    The settings are MPARAM(IM,86) (Profile Treatment) and MPARAM(IM,87) (Edge Diffraction).

    4. Edge diffraction¶

    4.1 What is implemented¶

    With Edge Diffraction on, every reflected ray receives a Fraunhofer deflection for the aperture the reflected beam sees. In the plane of incidence that is a slit of width L sin(theta), with L the mirror length and theta the local grazing angle; across it, a slit of the mirror width W. The deflection is drawn from sinc^2 by the same routine RAY-UI uses at slits (FRAUN_DIFFRACTION). On a grating the aperture in the dispersion plane is L sin(beta), with beta the grazing angle of the diffracted order (section 2.4).

    4.2 When it is right, and when not¶

    For a uniformly illuminated, overfilled aperture the ensemble of deflected rays is the Fraunhofer pattern of the aperture. Convolved with the geometric image, it is the partially coherent image. That is the case in which a ray tracer misses the diffraction limit, and in it the setting is exact.

    It has three limits:

    • Underfilled mirrors. The diffraction of a beam's own finite size is already in the rays, through the radiation size and divergence of the source. A mirror that does not clip the beam adds nothing, but the setting still deflects every ray for the full aperture. QMAP M6 is filled to 175 of its 200 mm at 400 eV and to 117 mm at 1000 eV. There the setting broadens the horizontal focus, in FWHM / EE80, by 22 / 38 % and 10 / 17 %, while SRW agrees with the trace without it at 1000 eV (section 6.2). Switch the setting on only for mirrors that clip the beam.
    • Mirrors as long as their image distance. Each ray is deflected where it leaves the mirror and then travels its own distance to the image; for M6, with L = q = 200 mm, between 100 and 300 mm. The pattern at the image is therefore a mixture of sinc^2 patterns scaled by these distances. The traced rms broadening in the plane of incidence matches that of a single sinc^2 at 200 mm within 6 % at 50 to 200 eV. The enclosed energies come out right, EE80 and EE90 within 2 and 5 % of SRW at 50 eV. The rays from the near end, however, sharpen the peak, and the FWHM of the focus is too small, by 28 % at 50 eV. A mixture computed from the sinc^2 alone reproduces this: FWHM 262 nm instead of 315 nm for a single distance of 200 mm, but EE90 571 instead of 509 nm. For the usual L << q all rays travel nearly the same distance and the effect vanishes.
    • Mirrors strongly curved across the beam. The deflection is applied in the local plane of incidence. Where the beam overfills a mirror whose sagittal radius is comparable to its width, that plane tilts across the mirror and part of the in-plane deflection appears across it. M6 has sagittal radii of 18 to 30 mm on a 50 mm width. At 50 eV its vertical focus broadens by 151 nm rms, where the 50 mm width alone gives 71 nm. The excess shrinks with the sagittal footprint and is gone at 400 eV.

    4.3 Why not a deflection that depends on the distance to the edge¶

    Monte Carlo codes sometimes deflect a ray according to its distance d from the nearest edge, by a Gaussian of width about lambda / (4 pi d). This is the "Heisenberg uncertainty principle" method of Freniere, Gregory and Hassler [8]. It would leave underfilled mirrors alone. For a uniformly illuminated slit, however, it cannot give the Fraunhofer pattern. Once its FWHM is matched, 80 % and 90 % of the power lie within 1.7 and 3.0 lambda / D instead of 1.03 and 1.46.

    The reason is that the exact ray description of an aperture, its Wigner function [9], is sin(2 k theta d) / (pi theta). That has the lambda / d width of the uncertainty argument, but it takes negative values. Averaged over the aperture it gives sinc^2 exactly. No positive kernel can do the same, because the zeros of sinc^2 are interference. A position-independent Fraunhofer deflection is exact where edge diffraction matters, which is why it is used here. The complete treatment propagates a wavefront through the element, as the HYBRID method of SHADOW does [10].

    5. How to use it¶

    5.1 In the parameter panel¶

    For a mirror or grating with a measured or modelled profile:

    1. Slope Error: Yes.
    2. Surface Profile: Profile-File, and Filepath to the profile.
    3. Profile Treatment: wave-optical (PSD scattering).
    4. Edge Diffraction (Fraunhofer): on if the mirror clips the beam, otherwise off.

    Profile Treatment is enabled only for a profile file. The thermal bump and the cylindrical bowing are smooth figure shapes and stay geometric. Edge Diffraction is independent of the surface-error settings.

    The profile file is the format RAY-UI already reads. The first line holds the numbers of points across (x) and along (z) the mirror, then the spacings in mm; then, for each x, a line of the z values. Heights are in nm for the S and Z kinds and in mm for A (ANSYS). At most 251 x 2048 points are used; the mirror size is taken from the profile.

    5.2 In an RML file¶

    <param id="slopeError" comment="Yes" enabled="T">0</param>
<param id="profileKind" comment="Profile-File" enabled="T">0</param>
<param id="profileFile" enabled="T">/path/to/s_mirror_profile.dat</param>
<param id="surfaceErrorTreatment" comment="wave-optical (PSD scattering)" enabled="T">1</param>
<param id="edgeDiffraction" comment="on" enabled="T">1</param>

    Both ids can be scanned in a Looper preset like any other parameter. That is the easiest way to compare the two treatments on one beamline.

    5.3 Reading the result¶

    • Use FWHM and encircled energy, not RMS. The rms width barely changes between the treatments (section 2.2). The halo carries the same angular variance as the geometric blur, only in few rays.
    • Enough rays for the halo. At 1000 eV about 3 % of the rays reflected by QMAP M6 are scattered, and they spread over a few micrometres around a 40 nm core. Halo shapes need correspondingly many rays; FWHM and encircled energies converge like any other trace.
    • Coating roughness. The Névot–Croce roughness of a coating [11] lowers the reflectivity for microroughness below the resolution of a map. It is a separate input; do not enter the roughness a profile already contains as coating roughness as well.

    6. Validation¶

    6.1 The statistics of the implementation¶

    test/unit/surfacescattering/test_surfscat.f90 drives SURFSCAT_SETUP and SURFSCAT_APPLY directly. It uses a periodic, Gaussian-correlated profile of 1024 x 128 points with 0.25 nm rms, 4° grazing incidence and 400 000 rays per case. One run gave:

    check result expected
    sigma_h of the table 0.25000 nm 0.25000 nm
    specular fraction, mu = 0.1 0.90467 exp(-0.1) = 0.90484
    halo rms of the scattered rays, mu = 0.1 3.242e-7 rad 3.244e-7 rad, from the PSD
    rms deflection in plane, mu = 0.1, 30, 400 1.0009e-7, 1.0007e-7, 0.9999e-7 rad 2 m_z = 1.0008e-7 rad
    rms deflection across, mu = 0.1, 30, 400 1.785e-8, 1.786e-8, 1.781e-8 rad 2 sin(theta) m_x = 1.783e-8 rad
    edge diffraction FWHM, in plane / across 1.025 / 1.025 1, i.e. 0.886 lambda / D
    grating, alpha = 0.87°, beta = 1.95°: specular fraction, mu = 0.3 0.7428; six further runs 0.7393 to 0.7413 exp(-0.3) = 0.7408
    grating: edge diffraction FWHM with L sin(beta) 1.025 1

    The FWHM is read from a histogram with a bin of 0.025 of the expected width. The rms deflection is the same at every phase error, as section 2.2 states, and equals twice the slope the table's PSD implies. From finite differences of the map the slope comes out about 2.5 % different, because of the window and the discretisation; that is a property of the slope estimate, not of the scattering. The test exits with status 1 if a check leaves its tolerance of about four standard errors; eight repeated runs passed.

    6.2 Against SRW¶

    The QMAP beamline of section 1 was traced with ideal mirrors up to M4 and the synthetic directional-PSD maps of M5 and M6 as profile files: a Gaussian undulator source with a 0.07 % band, 10^6 rays per point. Four variants differ only in the two settings on M5 and M6. The Focus and exit-slit rays were reduced with the same metrics as the SRW intensities: - the FWHM around the highest peak, from a histogram with a bin of 1/20 of the 50 % width, smoothed 1-2-1; - the narrowest intervals holding 80 and 90 % of the power, EE80 and EE90.

    The SRW reference at 400 and 1000 eV is the 0.07 % band with 328 macro-electrons. At 50 to 150 eV it is monochromatic with 64 macro-electrons. Those low-energy runs predate a phase fix in SRW's focal transforms that moved the illumination of M6, so they are provisional, and only the horizontal axis is compared there.

    Regression. With both settings at their defaults, this branch traces bit-identically to dev: the same Focus rays, checksum included, at 50 eV with 400 000 rays.

    • Overfilled M6, low energy. The wave-optical treatment alone is too narrow, because it removes the slope blur and adds no diffraction.     Exit slit. The two settings act only on M5 and M6. The exit slit is identical in all variants: 14.9 to 15.0 um vertically against 15.1 um in SRW. Horizontally, RAY-UI is already wider at the slit at low energy: 25.8 against 17.1 um at 50 eV, 14.0 against 10.0 um at 150 eV, and 5.9 against 6.1 um at 1000 eV. That comes from the source model, not from the mirrors, and it enters the horizontal focus from 50 to 150 eV.

    Statistics. 8 000 of the 10^6 rays reach the focus at 50 eV and 105 000 at 1000 eV. Repeated traces of the same variant are identical, so the scatter between neighbouring energies is the only measure of the ray noise. At 50 eV it is a few percent in the enclosed energies.

    Recommendation. - Use the wave-optical treatment for every mirror with a measured or modelled profile. - Switch edge diffraction on only for mirrors whose footprint fills their length, as M6 up to 200 eV. - Where a mirror clips only the tails of the beam, the traces with and without edge diffraction bracket the result.

    6.3 Gratings in a trace¶

    The RAY-UI test beamline test/scripting/GratingEfficiencyTest.rml was changed to a collimated, monochromatic 400 eV beam that overfills its 200 x 50 mm plane grating: 150 l/mm, grazing incidence 0.867° and exit 1.950°. Without surface errors every diffracted ray leaves in the same direction. The fraction of rays still in that direction, and the angular spread, can therefore be measured directly at the image plane, with 10^6 rays traced and 95 000 arriving.

    check trace expected
    specular fraction, wave-optical, synthetic profile with sigma_h = 7 nm 0.6129 ± 0.0016 exp(-mu) = 0.6147; with beta alone, as on a mirror, 0.393
    angular FWHM in the dispersion plane, edge diffraction on 1.000 1, i.e. 0.886 lambda / (L sin(beta))
    angular FWHM across it 1.000 1, i.e. 0.886 lambda / W
    both settings off no spread none

    The widths come from a histogram with a bin of 1/40 of the expected width.

    With both settings at their defaults the three grating routines trace bit-identically to dev: - QMAP, with its plane grating, at 50 eV; - RAYpy/rml/beamline.rml, with a spherical grating; - the same beamline with its grating loaded as a toroidal grating.

    With edge diffraction on, the same beamlines keep their ray counts and change their directions. The mirror traces of section 6.2 are also bit-identical before and after the grating routine was split off.

    The gratings have not yet been compared with a wave-optics code.

    7. Assumptions and limits¶

    • Statistics, not the local patch. The table describes the whole map as a stationary Gaussian process. A ray scatters according to the statistics of the surface, not according to the particular patch it hits. A single deterministic feature, such as a bump or a bend, is averaged into the halo. Deterministic figure shapes belong in the geometric treatment.
    • Kirchhoff approximation. The treatment is scalar, with small slopes and without shadowing or multiple scattering on the surface. Grazing-incidence mirrors satisfy all of these.
    • Reflectivity. A scattered ray keeps the reflectivity computed for the specular direction, and on a grating the efficiency of its order.
    • Grating mounts. The edge deflection uses the apertures L sin(beta) and W about the diffracted ray. That is exact when the ray lies in the plane of the grating length, as in the classical in-plane mount. In off-plane and conical mounts (REFRAC2D) the projected aperture is a parallelogram, and the separable deflection is an approximation. The reflection zone plate (RZP), whose two-step diffraction differs from a grating's, is not included.
    • Resolution of the map. Spatial periods longer than the map or shorter than twice its spacing are missing from the PSD.
    • Edge diffraction. See section 4.2.
    • Threads and reproducibility. The tables are module state and the draws use the Fortran random_number generator. Both are safe because RAY-UI traces optical elements single-threaded: no mirror sets isThreadSafe, so the OpenMP ray loop of the renderer never runs over them. A mirror marked thread-safe would need per-thread generators. A given beamline, ray number and build traces to identical rays on every run.
